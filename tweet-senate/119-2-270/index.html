Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026

The poll got no votes, and the Senate passed the bill 62 Yea, 33 Nay

The poll on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 ended 0 Yea, 0 Nay, so it has no majority. The Senate voted Yea and passed the bill 62 Yea, 33 Nay.

The original poll on X

The day before this vote, the Senate replaced all of the text of H.R. 2347 with a new bill of about 70,000 words. Most of it is about permits, the government’s OK to build things like pipelines, power lines and mines. Some projects have every federal permit they need and a backer that is not the federal government. For these projects, agencies could take back or greatly change permits in effect on or after November 6, 2026 only in six cases. If the permit holder sues, the agency must prove that one of the six cases applies. If it cannot, and the holder shows the agency acted in bad faith, the court would award 25 to 50 percent of what the holder had spent to develop and build the project up to the agency’s action. Costs already paid back as lawyer fees or delay costs would not count. Federal agencies would get deadlines to finish their environmental studies. A suit over a project’s NEPA environmental review generally would have to be filed within 150 days. The clock starts when the approval is published in the Federal Register or made public. Federal law can set a shorter time. For a NEPA violation, courts could send an approval back but not cancel it. Clean Water Act pollution permits could last up to 10 years instead of 5. The usual deadline for endangered species checks would drop from 90 to 60 days, with extensions allowed. Data centers that connect on or after the day the law is enacted would have to pay the extra power costs they cause. Arrangements a state approved earlier would stay. The bill also changes rules for power lines, energy on federal land, nuclear licenses and historic sites. It keeps the tax break that the original H.R. 2347 was written to create. The break is for damages received over a sexual act or sexual contact, except punitive damages. The break applies to decisions and agreements made after the law is enacted, with timing rules for older ones.

The Senate passed the bill by 62 votes to 33 on November 19, 2026. Republicans voted 47 to 2 for it, Democrats 14 to 30, and independents 1 to 1. Five senators did not vote. The day before, the Senate had voted 69 to 27 to put this text in place of the bill’s old text.

The poll on X summed it up like this and asked how you would vote.

Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act: Speed up pipeline, power line and mine permits. If its backer isn't the federal government, a project with all its federal permits could lose those in effect on or after Nov. 6, 2026 only in six cases.

The replies in the thread gave more detail and the case for and against.

Agencies could not cancel, pause or greatly change such a permit or do anything else to stop the project from being built or run. Four cases are a court order, a request or a serious breach of the permit or law by the holder, and a law or rule that applies.

The other two are a permit won by fraud, hiding facts on purpose or misstating key facts, and a step needed to prevent harm that is immediate, specific, urgent, substantial and proximate, based on new facts not weighed in the permit's review or approval.

In court, the agency must prove one of the six applies. If not, the holder gets the permit back and may get lawyer fees and delay costs. Bad faith adds 25% to 50% of the holder's development and building costs up to the agency's action, less what those cover.

The Energy Secretary must start a Data Center Community Assistance Program. If state, local or tribal governments ask, it gives them facts and expert advice on a planned data center. That can include its power and water use and who would pay for grid upgrades.

The Senate passed it 62-33 on Nov. 19. Republicans voted 47-2, Democrats 14-30 and independents 1-1. Sen. Jeff Merkley (D-OR) objected to a vote on a managers' amendment from the bill's four authors, so the bill passed without it.

Sen. Mike Lee (R-UT) said the bill is about "the freedom to build." Sen. Tina Smith (D-MN) said it "would make it nearly impossible for future administrations to take necessary actions to regulate or rescind approvals" for a mine near the Boundary Waters.

The bill, part by part

The bill the Senate passed is about 70,000 words long, in three parts called divisions. Many of its sections rewrite older laws, such as the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), the Clean Water Act and the Endangered Species Act. So a part like 1110(b) means part (b) of the new words that section 1110 puts into the older law. This page shows parts of sections 1106 and 1110 (NEPA deadlines and lawsuits), 1203 and 1204 (Clean Water Act permits), 1302 (endangered species), 1401 (keeping permits in force), 2107 and 2117 (data centers) and 3001 (the tax break). It leaves out the rest of Division A, which also covers the Council on Environmental Quality, state water quality approvals, states taking over species reviews, nuclear licenses, wildfire emissions and brownfields. It leaves out the rest of Division B, which covers power lines and the grid, energy leasing on federal land, geothermal power, water power, offshore energy, historic preservation and more. The words shown are amendment 6883 as it was proposed on November 10, 2026, printed in the Congressional Record. The Senate adopted it on November 18 in place of the bill’s old text. It set aside two other amendments, 6884 and 6886, that day. On November 19 an objection blocked a vote on a managers’ amendment, 7030, and the Senate then passed the bill with no other changes. The full text is at govinfo.gov.

Each part below has a short note in plain words and a line or two of the bill’s own words in quotation marks. Open “The bill’s words” under a note to read that whole part.

Section 1106. TIMELY AND UNIFIED FEDERAL REVIEWS

Big environmental studies get 2 years

This section rewrites part of NEPA. NEPA makes federal agencies study and tell the public how a big project could affect the environment. The biggest of these studies is called an environmental impact statement. The agency in charge, called the lead agency, must publish it within 2 years. The clock starts on the earlier of two dates. One is the day the agency decides the study is needed. The other is the day it tells the project’s backer that the application is complete. The bill calls that backer the project sponsor. Paragraph (2), not shown here, allows a longer deadline in some cases. Smaller studies, called environmental assessments, get 1 year.

“the lead agency shall publish an environmental impact statement”

The bill’s words, section 1106(4)(A)(1)(B)(i)
(i) In general.—Except as provided in paragraph (2), with respect to a proposed agency action, the lead agency shall publish an environmental impact statement, if applicable, not later than the date that is 2 years after the earlier of, as applicable—(I) the date on which the lead agency determines that section 102(2)(C) requires the issuance of an environmental impact statement with respect to the proposed agency action; and(II) the date on which the lead agency notifies the project sponsor that the application for the proposed agency action is complete.

Some agency heads face travel limits after 60 days of delay

Say an agency the bill calls a key permitting agency misses its deadline by 60 days. It has not published or made public the environmental document. It also has not issued all outstanding authorizations. Then the head of that agency may not travel more than 25 miles from their usual workplace on official business. This lasts until the agency has done both. For the Army Corps of Engineers, the head is the Assistant Secretary of the Army for Civil Works. Another part says this rule does not apply to the Secretary of Defense.

“shall not travel more than 25 miles from their official duty station for the purposes of official business”

The bill’s words, section 1106(4)(C)(3)(B)
(B) No travel outside duty station.—(i) In general.—If, after 60 days have passed from the date on which a deadline described in paragraph (1) or (2) was failed to be met and the key permitting agency has not published or made public the applicable environmental document and has not issued all outstanding authorizations, as described in those paragraphs, with respect to a proposed agency action, the head of the relevant key permitting agency shall not travel more than 25 miles from their official duty station for the purposes of official business until the key permitting agency has published or made public the applicable environmental document and issued all outstanding authorizations related to the proposed agency action that are not in compliance with the requirements of this subsection.(ii) Corps of engineers.—For purposes of clause (i), the Assistant Secretary of the Army for Civil Works shall be considered to be the head of the Corps of Engineers.

Project backers can sue over missed deadlines

The project sponsor may ask a court to rule on whether an agency met its deadline. Other parts of this paragraph set the rules for that case. The court must decide within 120 days after the case is filed, unless there are extraordinary circumstances. If the sponsor wins, the court must order a decision on the application within 60 days. An agency is excused only if it proves with clear and convincing evidence that the delay had a listed cause. Two of the causes are a natural disaster and a gap in government funding of more than 2 weeks.

“A project sponsor may obtain judicial review of the compliance by a Federal agency with the relevant deadline”

The bill’s words, section 1106(4)(C)(4)(B)
(B) Right to petition.—A project sponsor may obtain judicial review of the compliance by a Federal agency with the relevant deadline established under paragraph (1) or (2) by filing a written petition in a court of competent jurisdiction.

Section 1110. JUDICIAL REVIEW

150 days to sue, and only for some people

This section adds new rules to NEPA for lawsuits that say an agency did its review wrong. These rules apply to claims filed on or after the day the law is enacted. A lawsuit like this is barred unless it passes three tests. First, it must be filed within 150 days. Usually, the clock starts when the approval is published in the Federal Register, if an impact statement was prepared. In other cases it starts when the approval is made public. For a supplemental or revised environmental document ordered by a court, a separate rule starts the clock when the approval is made public. Another federal law can set a shorter time. Second, if the public was asked for comments, the person suing must have sent one. The comment must be detailed enough to warn the agency about the same issue. This applies to comment periods after the law takes effect. Third, the person suing, or an individual member of a group that is suing, must claim a real harm that has happened or is about to happen.

“is filed not later than 150 days after the date on which, unless a shorter deadline is specified under Federal law—”

“an actual or imminent injury in fact amounting to a direct harm”

The bill’s words, section 1110(b)
(b) Limitations on Claims.—Notwithstanding any other provision of law, a claim shall be barred unless the claim—(1) is filed not later than 150 days after the date on which, unless a shorter deadline is specified under Federal law—(A) in the case of a claim in which an environmental impact statement was prepared, the applicable authorization was published in the Federal Register; and(B) in the case of any other claim, the authorization was made public;(2) in the case of an authorization for which an opportunity for comment was provided, and for which the opportunity for public comment occurs after the date of enactment of the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026, the claim is filed by a party that submitted a substantive comment during a public comment period associated with that authorization, with such comment being sufficiently detailed to put the applicable Federal agency on notice of the particular issue on which the party seeks judicial review; and(3) is filed by a party alleging (or has an individual member alleging) an actual or imminent injury in fact amounting to a direct harm.

Courts cannot cancel approvals to remedy NEPA violations

If a court finds that an approval broke NEPA’s rules, it may only remand it. That means send it back to the agency to fix. The court may not vacate the approval, which means cancel it. It may not issue an injunction, an order to stop work. The court must say what to fix and give the agency no more than 180 days to act. There is one exception. A court may still pause a project for a time with a preliminary injunction. But only if there is a substantial likelihood that the approval violates another federal law, excluding NEPA and division A of subtitle III of title 54 of the U.S. Code. Three more tests must be met, such as stopping harm that could not be undone.

“the only remedy the court may order to redress that violation is to remand, without vacatur or issuance of an injunction”

The bill’s words, section 1110(c)(5)
(5) Remedy.—(A) In general.—Except as provided in subparagraph (B) and notwithstanding any other provision of law, if a court determines that an authorization does not comply with the requirements of this Act, the only remedy the court may order to redress that violation is to remand, without vacatur or issuance of an injunction, the applicable authorization to the agency with—(i) specific instructions to correct errors or deficiencies found by the court; and(ii) a reasonable schedule and deadline for the agency to act on remand, which shall not exceed 180 days after the date on which the order of the court was entered.(B) Preliminary injunctions.—(i) In general.—Notwithstanding subparagraph (A) and subject to clause (ii), a court may issue a preliminary injunction for the applicable authorization when—(I) there is a substantial likelihood the authorization violates a Federal law other than—(aa) this Act; or(bb) division A of subtitle III of title 54, United States Code;(II) necessary to prevent irreparable harm to the plaintiff;(III) the balance of equities favors the plaintiff; and(IV) a preliminary injunction is in the public interest.(ii) Requirements.—A preliminary injunction issued by a court under clause (i) shall last no longer than the time the court finds is reasonably necessary—(I) for the correction of the violation described in clause (i)(I); and(II) to prevent the irreparable harm described in clause (i)(II).

Work can go on while the agency fixes mistakes

When a court sends an approval back, the approval stays in force while the agency fixes the problems the court found. The project may also go ahead during that time.

“shall remain in effect while the Federal agency corrects any errors or deficiencies found by the reviewing court”

The bill’s words, section 1110(c)(6)
(6) Remanded actions.—(A) Continued effect of authorization.—An authorization remanded under paragraph (5)(A) shall remain in effect while the Federal agency corrects any errors or deficiencies found by the reviewing court.(B) Federal action during remand.—The activity that is the subject of an authorization remanded under paragraph (5)(A) may, notwithstanding that remand, be carried out, including during the time prescribed by the applicable court to correct the applicable errors or deficiencies.

Section 1203. PERMIT TERM FOR NPDES PERMITS

Water pollution permits can last up to 10 years, not 5

The Clean Water Act lets the government give permits to discharge, or release, pollutants into the nation’s waters. These are called NPDES permits. NPDES stands for National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System. Today the law says these permits last no more than five years. This change makes the limit 10 years, so a permit could last up to 10 years. The law already applies the same limits to permits the federal government gives as to permits from states.

“by striking “five years” and inserting “10 years””

The bill’s words, section 1203
Section 402(b)(1)(B) of the Federal Water Pollution Control Act (33 U.S.C. 1342(b)(1)(B)) is amended by striking “five years” and inserting “10 years”.

Section 1204. PERMITS FOR DREDGED OR FILL MATERIAL

General permits: filling under 2 acres counts as small harm

The Army Corps of Engineers gives permits to put dredged material or fill into the nation’s waters. Dredged material is dug up from the bottom. Fill is material put in to fill an area. For similar kinds of work that cause only minimal harm, separately and added together, the Corps may give a general permit. One general permit covers many similar projects, so each one does not need its own. When the Corps judges the harm of this work, this part sets two rules. It may look only at how the work affects water quality rules. And it must count filling in less than 2 acres of water as only a minimal harm.

“shall consider any effects of a discharge of dredged or fill material into less than 2 acres of navigable waters to be a minimal adverse environmental effect”

The bill’s words, section 1204(b)(3)(3)
(3) Considerations.—In determining the environmental effects of an activity under paragraph (1) or (2), the Secretary—(A) shall consider only the effects on compliance with water quality requirements (as defined in section 401(h)) of any discharge of dredged or fill material resulting from the activity; and(B) shall consider any effects of a discharge of dredged or fill material into less than 2 acres of navigable waters to be a minimal adverse environmental effect.

Section 1302. TIMELINE FOR SECTION 7 CONSULTATIONS

Endangered species checks get 60 days, not 90

The Endangered Species Act makes each federal agency check with the Secretary of the Interior or of Commerce before it acts, unless an exemption applies. The goal is to make sure the action is not likely to threaten the survival of an endangered or threatened species. It also must not be likely to destroy or harm habitat officially named as critical. This check is called consultation. Today it must end within 90 days, unless both sides agree to more time. This cuts it to 60 days, with the same room to agree to more. Some checks involve a permit or license applicant. Then a first proposed period longer than 60 days but shorter than 100 days needs a written statement to the applicant before the end of day 60. It must give the reasons, the information still needed and the estimated finish date. A first proposed period of 100 days or more needs the applicant’s consent before the end of day 60. The agencies may later agree to extend a set period if the Secretary gets the applicant’s consent before that period ends. Today those marks are 90 and 150 days. If the proposed period runs past 145 days and the statement or consent was not given, the applicant may seek relief in court. For an action that needs a NEPA environmental document, consultation must also finish no later than the day that document is completed. This rule does not extend any earlier consultation deadline. The Secretary may approve a longer time only for extraordinary circumstances that the agencies could not control and did not cause.

“by striking “90-day” and inserting “60-day””

The bill’s words, section 1302
Section 7(b)(1) of the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (16 U.S.C. 1536(b)(1)) is amended—(1) in subparagraph (A), by striking “90-day” and inserting “60-day”;(2) in subparagraph (B)—(A) in the matter preceding clause (i)—(i) by striking “90 days” and inserting “60 days”; and(ii) by striking “90th day” and inserting “60th day”;(B) in clause (i), in the matter preceding subclause (I), by striking “150th day” and inserting “100th day”;(C) in clause (ii), by striking “150 or more” and inserting “100 or more”; and(D) in the undesignated matter following clause (ii)—(i) by striking “the preceding sentence” and inserting “subparagraph (B)”; and(ii) by striking “The Secretary” and inserting the following:“(C) The Secretary”; and(3) by inserting after subparagraph (C) (as so designated) the following:“(D) If the consultation period proposed to be agreed to between the Secretary and the Federal agency under subparagraph (B) extends beyond 145 days and the Secretary and the Federal agency have not provided a written statement to, or obtained consent from, a permit or license applicant under clause (i) or (ii), as applicable, of that subparagraph during such period, the applicant shall be eligible for relief under section 706(1) of title 5, United States Code.“(E) (i) In the case of an agency action that requires the preparation of an environmental document (as defined in section 3 of the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969), the consultation process under subsection (a)(2) shall be completed by not later than the date on which the applicable environmental document (as so defined) for the agency action is completed in accordance with section 107(g) of the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 (42 U.S.C. 4336a(g)), unless the Secretary approves an extension of the deadline due to extraordinary circumstances beyond the control, and not due to any action, of the consulting parties.“(ii) The requirement under clause (i) shall not be construed to extend the deadlines described in subparagraphs (A) through (D).”.

Section 1401. MAINTAINING FEDERAL AUTHORIZATIONS OR PERMITS FOR PROJECTS WITH NON-FEDERAL SPONSORS

Which projects and permits this covers

This section covers a project whose backer is not the federal government. The project must already have every federal permit it needs to be built or to run. The bill uses the word permit in a broad way. It includes many kinds of federal approval, such as a right-of-way or a finding that a project will not have a big effect on the environment. It also includes an agency’s finding that no more permits are needed.

“has secured all necessary Federal authorizations or permits for construction or operation”

The bill’s words, section 1401(a)
(a) Definitions.—In this section:(1) Covered project or action.—The term “covered project or action” means a project or action with a non-Federal project sponsor that has secured all necessary Federal authorizations or permits for construction or operation.(2) Federal authorization or permit.—The term “Federal authorization or permit” means an authorization, permit, record of decision, right-of-way, finding of no significant impact, verification, extension, construction and operations plan, biological opinion, incidental take statement, concurrence, variance, departure, determination, or any other similar approval or order that is necessary, including any determination by a Federal department or agency that additional authorizations or permits are not necessary, for the construction or operation at normal capacity of a project.

Agencies may not undo these permits, except in six cases

For a covered project, a federal agency may not take a new step to cancel, pause, reopen or greatly change a permit. This covers permits in effect on or after November 6, 2026. The agency also may not take any other step to stop the project from being built or from running at full capacity. There are six exceptions. A court orders the step. The permit holder broke the permit’s terms or the law in a serious way. The permit was won by fraud, by hiding facts on purpose, or by misstating important facts. The step is needed to prevent harm that is immediate, specific, urgent, serious and proximate, which means close and direct. The harm can be to life, property, health, the environment, national security or defense. It must be based on new facts that were not weighed in the permit’s environmental review or final decision. The permit holder asks for the step. Or the step is taken under a law or rule that applies. A step taken to prevent harm must be no bigger or longer than needed. Before acting, the agency must tell the holder in writing what it will do, under what law, and on what evidence.

“a Federal department or agency shall not take any new action that would revoke”

“such action is taken pursuant to any applicable law or regulation”

The bill’s words, section 1401(b)(1)
(1) In general.—For covered projects or actions, a Federal department or agency shall not take any new action that would revoke, rescind, withdraw, terminate, suspend, vacate, remand, reopen, materially amend, or materially alter a Federal authorization or permit in effect on or after November 6, 2026, or take any other action to interfere with or prevent the construction or operation at normal capacity of a project that has secured all necessary Federal authorizations and permits, unless—(A) the Federal department or agency takes such action in response to an order by a court of competent jurisdiction;(B) the holder of the Federal authorization or permit has materially breached the terms of the Federal authorization or permit or otherwise materially violated applicable law;(C) the Federal authorization or permit was obtained through fraud, intentional concealment, or material misrepresentation;(D) such action is necessary to prevent immediate, specific, urgent, substantial, and proximate harm or damage to life, property, human health, the environment, national security, or defense that is based on new information that was not considered in the underlying environmental review process or final agency action for the Federal authorization or permit;(E) the Federal department or agency has received a request from the holder of the Federal authorization or permit for the covered project or action to take such action; or(F) such action is taken pursuant to any applicable law or regulation.

In court, the agency must prove an exception applies

The permit holder can ask a federal court to review a step an agency takes under this section. Unless another law sends the case straight to a federal court of appeals, the holder can sue in a district court where the project is at least partly located, or in Washington, D.C. The court must make the agency prove its case. That is called the burden of proof. The agency has to show that at least one of the six exceptions applies.

“place the burden of proof on the Federal department or agency”

The bill’s words, section 1401(b)(4)(B)
(B) Standard of review.—In reviewing an action taken by a Federal department or agency under paragraph (1) under subchapter II of chapter 5 and chapter 7 of title 5, United States Code (commonly known as the “Administrative Procedure Act”), or any other provision of law, the reviewing court shall, notwithstanding subchapter II of chapter 5 and chapter 7 of title 5, United States Code (commonly known as the “Administrative Procedure Act”), or any other provision of law, place the burden of proof on the Federal department or agency to demonstrate that 1 or more of the matters described in subparagraphs (A) through (F) of that paragraph applies.

Bad faith can add 25 to 50% of unrecovered project costs

If the agency cannot prove an exception, the holder gets the permit back. The agency must also stop any step that blocks the project. The holder is also entitled to a court order that bars agencies from taking a new step to cancel, pause, reopen or greatly change a permit already granted, or to stop the project from being built or running at full capacity. To take such a step, an agency must first get an order from the same judge. If that judge no longer sits in that district, another judge there can give the order. The court may also award lawyer fees and the costs of the delay. Bad faith means acting dishonestly or for an improper reason. If the holder shows an agency acted in bad faith, the court must add 25 to 50 percent of what the holder spent to develop and build the project up to the date of the agency’s step. Costs already paid back as lawyer fees or delay costs are left out. The court sets the amount within that range. It looks at how much bad faith there was, whether agencies did the same to other projects, and how much the project was harmed. Under part (F), the money comes from a standing federal fund that pays court judgments. It does not depend on Congress setting money aside. No payment can be made without a final judgment from a federal court.

“not less than 25 percent and not more than 50 percent of the total of all development and construction costs”

The bill’s words, section 1401(b)(4)(D)
(D) Relief.—(i) In general.—If, at the conclusion of its review of an action taken by a Federal department or agency under paragraph (1), the court concludes that the Federal department or agency has not met its burden under subparagraph (B), the holder of the Federal authorization or permit for a covered project or action shall be entitled to—(I) the reinstatement of the Federal authorization or permit and the termination of any action by the Federal department or agency preventing the construction or operation of the covered project or action;(II) injunctive relief preventing a Federal department or agency from taking any new action that would revoke, rescind, withdraw, terminate, suspend, vacate, remand, reopen, materially amend, or materially alter a previously granted Federal authorization or permit or take any other action to interfere with or prevent the construction or operation at normal capacity of the covered project or action without first obtaining a court order to that effect from—(aa) the same judge that heard the original matter, assuming that judge continues to sit in the same district; or(bb) if that judge is no longer sitting in the same district, from another judge in the same district;(III) reasonable attorney fees and costs of litigation, as the court determines appropriate;(IV) any costs associated with the applicable delay in the construction or operation of the covered project or action, as the court determines appropriate; and(V) on a showing that 1 or more Federal agencies interfered with a Federal authorization or permit in bad faith or interfered in bad faith with the construction or operation of a project that has secured all necessary Federal authorizations or permits, costs in an amount not less than 25 percent and not more than 50 percent of the total of all development and construction costs incurred by the holder of the Federal authorization or permit for a covered project or action in connection with the project as of the date of the action and not otherwise recovered under subclause (III) or (IV), which amount shall be determined by the court by taking into consideration—(aa) the degree to which the Federal interference in the project occurred due to bad faith;(bb) the degree to which there exists a pattern of similar Federal interference in other projects; and(cc) the actual degree of economic harm to the project sponsor or operator.(ii) Savings clause.—Nothing in this subparagraph limits or supersedes any existing equitable authority of a court to grant any available form of temporary or injunctive relief against the Federal department or agency.(iii) Motion for temporary or preliminary relief.—Notwithstanding any other provision of this section, on a motion for temporary or preliminary relief, the reviewing court shall not consider available cost recovery under subclause (III), (IV), or (V) of clause (i) in considering whether harm to the holder of a Federal authorization or permit is irreparable.

Section 2107. RATEPAYER PROTECTION

New data centers pay the extra power costs they cause

This adds a new section 229 to the Federal Power Act. It is about data centers and other sites used mainly for computing, such as for AI or cryptocurrency mining. The bill calls these computational load sites. A power company’s extra cost of serving one of these sites is called the retail incremental cost. It includes the power plants, storage and power lines needed to serve the site. Only the site’s owner or operator may be charged that cost. Other customers may not. A state may charge the site more and use the extra to lower other customers’ bills. If the site stops buying power early, it still must pay off that cost. Before building or upgrading a facility needed to serve the site, the power company must require reasonable financial assurances or contributions from the site’s owner or operator to cover the expected cost. It must credit any that the site already gave a public utility or grid operator for the same site or upgrade. This applies to sites that connect to the grid on or after the day the law is enacted. An arrangement a state approved before then stays in effect. A state may revise it to match these rules, to the extent state law allows.

“No portion of the retail incremental cost may be allocated to, or recovered in the rates of, any customer other than a computational load owner or a computational load operator.”

The bill’s words, section 2107(b)(b)
(b) Ratepayer Protection Floor.—(1) No subsidy of data centers.—No portion of the retail incremental cost may be allocated to, or recovered in the rates of, any customer other than a computational load owner or a computational load operator.(2) Surplus permitted.—Paragraph (1) does not limit the authority of a State under subsection (c)—(A) to recover from a computational load owner or computational load operator amounts in excess of the retail incremental cost; or(B) to credit those amounts to other customers as a rebate or a reduction to the electric utility costs that are used as a basis for the calculation of customer rates.(3) Continuing recovery on exit.—A rate, charge, or agreement for service to a computational load site shall be designed to recover from the computational load owner or computational load operator the full retail incremental cost of the electric utility, and such recovery shall continue notwithstanding the termination by the computational load owner or computational load operator of a contract or other agreement pertaining to the sale of electric energy, or the cessation by the computational load owner or computational load operator of the purchase of electric energy, before that retail incremental cost has been fully recovered.(4) Financial assurance.—Before constructing, or making an upgrade to, a generation, transmission, or distribution facility necessary to serve a computational load site, an electric utility shall require the computational load owner or computational load operator to provide reasonable financial assurances or contributions sufficient to cover the expected cost of, or upgrade to, the computational load site, subject to the condition that any financial assurances or contributions provided by the applicable computational load owner or computational load operator to a public utility or Transmission Organization for the same computational load site or upgrade shall be credited against the requirement of this paragraph.

Section 2117. DATA CENTER COMMUNITY ASSISTANCE PROGRAM

Help for towns weighing a data center

Within 180 days after the law is enacted, the Secretary of Energy must start the Data Center Community Assistance Program. State, local and tribal governments may ask it for help. It gives help only when they ask. It gives them facts and expert advice on how a planned data center could affect them. That covers energy, water, infrastructure, the local economy and more. Another part says the help can include how much power and water the site would use, who would pay for grid upgrades, and jobs and taxes.

“objective and technically sound information, resources, and technical assistance”

The bill’s words, section 2117(b)(1)
(1) In general.—Not later than 180 days after the date of enactment of this Act, the Secretary shall establish a program, to be known as the “Data Center Community Assistance Program”, under which the Secretary shall provide State, local, and Tribal governments, on request, with objective and technically sound information, resources, and technical assistance regarding the potential energy, water, infrastructure, economic, and other community impacts associated with computational load site development.

The Energy Secretary’s help must stay neutral

The Secretary must point out the guesses, limits and unknowns in any study the program gives. The Secretary may not tell a government whether to approve or reject a data center. Another part says nothing in the bill lets the Secretary take part in a state, local or tribal decision. That means a decision on where a data center goes or how it is built. That covers approving, rejecting, delaying or setting conditions.

“shall not make any recommendation to a State, local, or Tribal government regarding whether any particular computational load site should be approved, rejected, permitted, or otherwise authorized”

The bill’s words, section 2117(e)
(e) Neutrality.—In carrying out activities under this section, the Secretary—(1) shall clearly identify material assumptions, limitations, and uncertainties associated with any analysis provided; and(2) shall not make any recommendation to a State, local, or Tribal government regarding whether any particular computational load site should be approved, rejected, permitted, or otherwise authorized.

Section 3001. EXCLUSION FROM GROSS INCOME OF ANY DAMAGES, OTHER THAN PUNITIVE DAMAGES, RECEIVED ON ACCOUNT OF SEXUAL ACTS OR SEXUAL CONTACT

Damages over sexual acts: no income tax, except punitive

This division has the name of the original H.R. 2347, the Survivor Justice Tax Prevention Act. Damages are money paid to make up for a harm, through a court decision or a settlement. The tax code already leaves some damages out of taxable income. It covers money received “on account of personal physical injuries or physical sickness.” This adds money received because of a sexual act or sexual contact, as federal criminal law defines them. It counts whether or not there are medical records or visible injuries. The section’s title says it does not cover punitive damages, which are meant to punish. It applies to decisions and agreements made after the law is enacted. A decision counts as made later if its first payment comes after the law is enacted. An agreement that replaces, supersedes or revises one made on or before the day the law is enacted does not count as new.

“whether or not there are medical records or observable injuries of such act or contact”

The bill’s words, section 3001(a)
(a) In General.—Section 104(a)(2) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986 is amended by striking “on account of personal physical injuries or physical sickness;” and inserting “on account of—“(A) personal physical injuries or physical sickness, or“(B) any sexual act (as defined in paragraph (2) of section 2246 of title 18, United States Code, as in effect on the date of the enactment of this subparagraph) or sexual contact (as defined in paragraph (3) of such section, as so in effect), whether or not there are medical records or observable injuries of such act or contact;”.
