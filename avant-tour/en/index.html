The avant-tour: duels instead of a primary

By Eric Bolton

I propose that two presidential candidates face each other before an assembly of citizens drawn by lot, and that the loser stand down for the winner. The other candidates do not have to agree.

The problem

The first round of France’s presidential election is on April 18, 2027, and the second on May 2. Only the two candidates who lead the first round may stand in the second: that is Article 7 of the Constitution.

The latest Toluna Harris Interactive barometer for M6 and RTL (online survey, November 3 to 5, 2026; 2,101 registered voters; margin of error 1.4 to 3.1 points) tested five first-round line-ups. In all five, Marine Le Pen comes first, with 34 to 36 percent, and Jean-Luc Mélenchon second, with 18 or 19.

When Édouard Philippe and Gabriel Attal both run, the same poll gives Philippe 11 percent, Attal 6 and Raphaël Glucksmann 11. Together that is 28 percent, ten points more than Mélenchon. None of the three would reach the second round.

A poll five months before the vote is not a forecast, and the pollster says so itself. But the mechanism is familiar. On April 21, 2002, in a first round with sixteen candidates, Lionel Jospin was eliminated 194,600 votes behind Jean-Marie Le Pen.

I will say where I stand. I think a second round between Marine Le Pen and Jean-Luc Mélenchon would be the worst choice to put before the country. In this survey nearly one voter in two chooses another candidate; on May 2 that candidate would no longer be in the race.

Why the primary is not moving

On October 28, Gabriel Attal proposed holding a primaire des démocrates, a primary of democrats, by the end of January. On November 1, on TF1, Édouard Philippe answered that he did not believe this primary would take place; he called it a primary of division and a primary of confusion. Maël de Calan, the coordinator of his spokespeople, quoted by Public Sénat on November 6, set out what Philippe proposes instead: between Christmas and February, the candidates who trail, Philippe included, stand down for the one who is best placed.

So both camps accept the principle of a single candidacy, but they do not agree on the method. A primary needs every leading candidate to take part: one refusal is enough to block it. The rule of the best placed does not say who decides, while this poll puts Philippe and Glucksmann level or one or two points apart, inside the margin of error. Yet 66 percent of the French people Harris asked say they favour a primary of the centre and the right.

The duel pact

Two candidates, any two, sign a public pact. They commit to the same tests in front of the cameras: costing their programme, debating one on one, handling a live news file as a head of state would. They accept the vote of an assembly of citizens drawn by lot, who will have followed those tests. The loser stands down and calls on voters to back the winner.

France has already convened such an assembly. In 2019, to draw the 150 members of the Citizens’ Convention on Climate by lot, Harris Interactive called more than 255,000 telephone numbers; six criteria, including sex, age, education and place of residence, framed the make-up of the panel. I would add the 2022 presidential vote and interest in politics. The draw would be held in public, with a tool built for the purpose, such as Panelot. The citizens would stay anonymous if they wished and could vote from home.

The format can be repeated. The winner of one duel can sign a new pact with another candidate. A series of duels stands in for a primary, but each step needs only two signatures.

A worked example

Take, purely as an example, a duel between Gabriel Attal and Édouard Philippe. None of the people named here has been consulted, and none has agreed to anything.

Harris measured what happens when only one of the two runs. Philippe, as the only candidate of the centre, gets 14 percent, not 17: not all the voters of a candidate who stands down move to the one who stays. Glucksmann moves from 11 to 12, Mélenchon from 18 to 19. If Attal is the one who stays, he gets 10, and Glucksmann 13. Either way Mélenchon keeps a lead of at least five points: one duel would not be enough.

A second duel would set the winner against Raphaël Glucksmann. On paper, 14 and 12 make 26, against 19. No poll that I know of tests that line-up, and the transfer of votes from a centre-left electorate to a centre-right candidate would be far from complete. If only half of the loser’s voters follow, the result is 19 or 20 against 19, inside the margin of error. Duels make qualification possible without guaranteeing it.

The objections

Some will call it a manoeuvre against two candidates. The pact takes no one off the ballot but volunteers, it is open to any pair, and voters will decide on April 18. Candidates have always stood down for one another; what is new is a public rule, set before the result is known.

Another objection: 150 citizens are not the people. It is a jury, not an electorate, and a narrow vote proves little. But a poll cannot separate two candidates one point apart, and an open primary would take months and everyone’s consent.

Nothing guarantees that the loser will keep his word. The pact has no legal force. What binds the loser is that the pact is signed in public and that the defeat happened in front of everyone. The duels also have to be over before March 12, 2027, the deadline for filing the 500 sponsorships.

And some will say that reaching the second round is not winning it. Harris has Marine Le Pen winning 58 to 42 against Philippe, 60 to 40 against Glucksmann and 68 to 32 against Mélenchon. The avant-tour can change the choice offered in the second round, and it leaves one candidate nearly two months of campaigning without a direct rival.

That leaves the question of the law: speaking time, campaign accounts. I do not claim to have settled it. Lawyers have to look at it before anything is filmed.

What comes next

Three things, in order. Publish the full rule before inviting anyone: the tests, the draw, the vote, the commitment. Find an independent guarantor for the draw and the count, and a broadcaster. Then find two candidates.

The calendar is tight. Attal wants his primary by the end of January, Philippe, through Maël de Calan, proposes withdrawals by February, and the collection of sponsorships ends on March 12. Duels would therefore have to take place in January and February, which means drawing the members of the assembly by lot in December.

This text is a proposal. No candidate and no party has approved it. I am ready to help put it into practice.
