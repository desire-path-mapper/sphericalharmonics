Protect College Sports Act of 2026

This bill sets national rules for paying college athletes. Schools could pay their players for the use of their name, image and likeness. But each school’s total pay would have a cap, set by a 2025 court settlement. Players in Division I would report deals over $600. Players could switch schools once and play right away. In Divisions I and II they would have 5 years to play. Division I schools would pay players’ out-of-pocket medical costs for sports injuries. The NCAA, conferences (leagues of schools) and schools could enforce the cap and other rules without breaking antitrust law, which bans unfair limits on trade. And states could not overrule school rules on pay, transfers or who may play.

The Senate passed S. 4668, as changed on the floor, by 77 votes to 22 on September 28, 2026. Republicans voted 50 to 2 for it, Democrats 26 to 19, and independents 1 to 1. One senator did not vote. The bill went to the House on October 5, 2026.

The poll on X summed it up like this and asked how you would vote.

Protect College Sports Act: Pay college athletes, but cap it. Colleges could pay players directly, up to a set limit per school. The NCAA could enforce that limit without breaking antitrust law, and states could not override it.

The replies in the thread gave more detail, the case for and against, and the cost.

Schools, conferences and the NCAA could not stop athletes from earning money from their name, image and likeness (NIL), as in ads. Schools could pay athletes directly. Other deals would need a real business purpose. Division I athletes must report deals over $600.

A school and its donor groups could not pay athletes, in total, past a cap set by a 2025 court settlement. The Congressional Research Service says it was $20.5 million in 2025-26 and will rise. For 9 years schools could add $22.5 million a year to keep players.

Athletes could transfer once without sitting out, and some more than once. In Divisions I and II they would get 5 years to play. Division I schools must cover out-of-pocket costs of sports injuries in college, and for 5 years after for athletes who finish there.

The NCAA, conferences and schools would not break antitrust law, which bans unreasonable limits on trade, by enforcing the cap or the rules on eligibility, transfers and recruiting. States could not override school rules on NIL pay, transfers or eligibility.

The sponsor, Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX), said it "puts an end to the chaos plaguing college athletics" and "creates clear, enforceable rules for eligibility and transfers." It passed 77-22 on Sept. 28. Republicans voted 50-2, Democrats 26-19 and independents 1-1.

Opposing it, Sen. Chris Murphy (D-CT) said the bill "only caps" the money going to players and "does nothing to cap the money going to the coaches." The bill limits only which funds can pay a coach over $500,000 at top-earning schools. The House got it Oct. 5.

In July the Congressional Budget Office looked at an earlier version. It estimated about $7 million in federal spending, mostly for a study commission. It said the bill's rules would cost schools, conferences, agents and others hundreds of millions a year.

The bill, part by part

The bill is about 28,000 words long, in three parts called titles. This page shows the sections that set the main rules, word for word. Those are three definitions from section 100 (paragraphs 2, 19 and 22), sections 101, 106, 112, 113, 114, 118, 121 and 122 of Title I, and section 202(a) of Title II. It leaves out section 1 (the short title and contents), the other definitions, sections 102 to 105, 107 to 111, 115 to 117, 119, 120 and 123 to 129 of Title I, section 202(b) and the rest of Title II, and all of Title III. Title III sets up grants for broadband, media and sports broadcasting equipment at historically Black colleges and universities. The full text is at govinfo.gov.

Each part below has a short note in plain words and a line or two of the bill’s own words in quotation marks. Open “The bill’s words” under a note to read that whole part.

Section 100. Definitions

Who counts as tied to a school

This list decides who counts as tied to a school. The bill calls them an “associated entity.” It says this can include a collective, which it defines as a person or group that gives money or other help to a school’s players or sports program. No one on the list may pay players in a way that gets around the school’s pay cap (section 114). The list has five parts. First is any group that school staff know, or should know, exists in significant part to back one school’s teams or players, or to find deals only for its players. Second is anyone who is or was a member, worker, owner or agent of such a group. Third is anyone who has given more than $50,000 in all, over their lifetime, to the school or to such a group. Fourth is anyone who has helped recruit or keep players. Fifth is any business or group that the others own, control, run or are linked to. Companies whose shares trade on the stock market are left out of that last part.

“any individual or entity, including a collective”

“has contributed more than $50,000 over their lifetime to a particular institution”

The bill’s words, section 100(2)
(2) Associated entity.—The term “associated entity” means any individual or entity, including a collective, that—(A) is known, or was known, or should have been known to the athletics department staff of an institution to exist, in significant part, for the purpose of—(i) promoting or supporting a particular institution's intercollegiate athletics program or student athletes; or(ii) creating or identifying name, image, and likeness opportunities solely for a particular institution's student athletes;(B) is or was a member, employee, director, officer, owner, or agent of an individual or entity described in subparagraph (A);(C) directly or indirectly (including contributions by an affiliated entity, individual, or family member) has contributed more than $50,000 over their lifetime to a particular institution or to an individual or entity described in subparagraph (A);(D) has—(i) been directed or requested by an institution's athletic department staff to assist in the recruitment or retention of student athletes or prospective student athletes; or(ii) otherwise assisted in the recruitment or retention of student athletes or prospective student athletes; or(E) is owned, controlled, or operated by, or otherwise affiliated with the individuals or entities described in subparagraphs (A) through (D), other than a publicly traded corporation.

The pay cap comes from a court deal

The cap on what a school may pay its players is not a dollar amount written in this bill. It uses the “Benefits Pool Limit” from a 2025 court settlement. A settlement is a deal that ends a lawsuit. This one ended antitrust cases against the NCAA and the biggest conferences, which are leagues of schools. It is known as the House settlement. Antitrust law bans deals that unfairly limit trade. Changes made later under the settlement’s own rules count too. The Congressional Research Service says the limit was $20.5 million per school for the 2025-26 school year. That does not count scholarships, and the limit rises over the years the settlement runs. Under section 115 (not shown here), the cap ends if the settlement ends, unless Congress passes a law to keep it.

“the Benefits Pool Limit set forth in the Injunctive Relief Settlement Agreement”

The bill’s words, section 100(19)
(19) Revenue share cap.—The term “revenue share cap” means the Benefits Pool Limit set forth in the Injunctive Relief Settlement Agreement approved by the court in “In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation”, No. 20–cv–03919 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2025), or as modified pursuant to the amendment provision specified in paragraph 55 of that settlement.

What a real business reason means

Deals with anyone other than the school must have a “valid business purpose” (section 114(d)). This says what that means. The deal must truly be about promoting goods or services that are sold to the public for profit.

“genuinely related to the promotion of goods or services provided to the general public for profit”

The bill’s words, section 100(22)
(22) Valid business purpose.—The term “valid business purpose” means a purpose genuinely related to the promotion of goods or services provided to the general public for profit.

Section 101. Name, image, and likeness protections

Players may earn money from their name and image

This is the main right in the bill. NIL stands for name, image and likeness. An NIL deal pays a player when their name, face or fame is used to sell or promote something. Schools, conferences and groups like the NCAA may not stop players or recruits from making NIL deals. Nor may anyone acting for them. They may not keep a player out of games because of a deal. Unless another law requires it, they also may not cut, limit or refuse to renew a player’s athletic scholarship because of a deal. The bill calls that scholarship a “grant-in-aid.” The words “except as provided in this title” mean the other limits in Title I, such as the pay cap, still apply.

“to market or earn compensation for the value of their name, image, or likeness rights”

“revoke, reduce, or decline to renew a grant-in-aid”

The bill’s words, section 101(a)(1)
(a) Student athlete name, image, and likeness compensation.—(1) In general.—Except as provided in this title, an institution, conference, intercollegiate athletic association, or any representative of such an entity may not—(A) restrict the ability of a student athlete, group of student athletes, or prospective student athlete—(i) to market or earn compensation for the value of their name, image, or likeness rights; or(ii) to enter into a name, image, and likeness agreement;(B) restrict the eligibility for intercollegiate athletic competition for a student athlete or prospective student athlete on the basis of the student athlete or prospective student athlete entering into a name, image, and likeness agreement or marketing or earning compensation for the value of their name, image, or likeness;(C) unless otherwise required by law, limit the eligibility or opportunity of a student athlete or prospective student athlete to apply for or receive a grant-in-aid, including the amount, duration, or renewal of such grant-in-aid, on the basis of the student athlete or prospective student athlete entering into a name, image, and likeness agreement, or marketing or earning compensation for the value of their name, image, or likeness; or(D) unless otherwise required by law, revoke, reduce, or decline to renew a grant-in-aid for a student athlete or prospective student athlete based on the student athlete or prospective student athlete entering into a name, image, and likeness agreement or marketing or earning compensation for the value of their name, image, or likeness.

Group deals need every player’s OK

No one may use the name, image or likeness of a group of players to sell or promote something unless each player in the group agrees. This covers schools, conferences, groups like the NCAA, booster groups and outside businesses.

“obtains an agreement from each member of the group for that purpose”

The bill’s words, section 101(a)(2)
(2) Consent and compensation for group use.—An institution, conference, intercollegiate athletic association, collective, third party, or any representative thereof, may not use the name, image, or likeness of any group of student athletes to sell or promote any product or service unless the institution, conference, athletic association, collective, or third party, as the case may be, obtains an agreement from each member of the group for that purpose.

When a school may keep a player out of games

There are two exceptions to the rule above. A school may keep a player out of games if the player’s deal breaks the school’s code of conduct for all its students. A school may also keep a player out of games if the player uses the school’s buildings, uniforms, gear, logos, trademarks or copyrighted work in a deal. That applies only when the school has not clearly said yes.

“without the express consent of the institution”

The bill’s words, section 101(a)(3)
(3) Exceptions.—(A) Certain agreements.—An institution may restrict the eligibility for intercollegiate athletic competition of a student athlete or prospective student athlete who enters into a name, image, and likeness agreement that violates the code of student conduct of the institution that applies to all students enrolled at the institution.(B) Certain uses.—An institution may restrict the eligibility for intercollegiate athletic competition of a student athlete or prospective student athlete if, in connection with a name, image, and likeness agreement, the student athlete or prospective student athlete uses a facility, uniform, equipment, registered or unregistered trademark, copyright-protected product, or the official logo, mark, or other indicia of the institution without the express consent of the institution.

Players must report deals over $600

Players must report each NIL deal worth more than $600. Payments from the same source over 12 months add up toward the $600. The report goes to the player’s school. It also goes to their college sports association, such as the NCAA, or to a group the association names and controls under the House settlement. A player must report a deal’s terms, including the amount and who is paying, within 5 days of signing it. Any pay not reported that way must be reported within 30 days of getting it.

“not later than 5 days after entering into a name, image, and likeness agreement”

“that exceeds $600 in value”

The bill’s words, section 101(b)(1)
(b) Mandatory disclosures by student athletes.—(1) In general.—All student athletes shall report to their institution and their intercollegiate athletic association, or to an entity designated by their intercollegiate athletic association and under the control of that intercollegiate athletic association pursuant to the Injunctive Relief Settlement Agreement approved by the court in “In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation”, No. 20–cv–03919 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2025)—(A) not later than 5 days after entering into a name, image, and likeness agreement, the terms of any such agreement, including the amount and source, that exceeds $600 in value, including multiple payments, remunerations, or benefits from the same entity that exceeds a total of $600 over a 12-month period; and(B) to the extent not reported under subparagraph (A), not later than 30 days after receiving compensation for the name, image, or likeness of the student athlete, the amount and source of any such compensation that exceeds $600, including multiple payments, remunerations, or benefits from the same entity that exceeds a total of $600 over a 12-month period.

Recruits report their deals too

A recruit who has not yet enrolled must also report. The report covers any current deal worth more than $600, counting all payments from the same source over 12 months. It goes to the school where the recruit enrolls. It also goes to the college sports association, or to the group the association names and controls.

“is recruited to attend, but is not yet enrolled in, an institution”

The bill’s words, section 101(b)(2)
(2) Mandatory disclosures by recruited athletes.—With respect to a student athlete or prospective student athlete who is recruited to attend, but is not yet enrolled in, an institution, the student athlete or prospective student athlete shall report to the institution at which the student athlete enrolls and their intercollegiate athletic association, or to an entity designated by that intercollegiate athletic association and under the control of that intercollegiate athletic association pursuant to the Injunctive Relief Settlement Agreement approved by the court in “In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation”, No. 20–cv–03919 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2025)—(A) the terms of any current or ongoing name, image, and likeness agreement that exceeds $600 in value, including multiple payments, remunerations, or benefits from the same entity that exceeds a total of $600 over a 12-month period; and(B) to the extent not reported under subparagraph (A), the amount and source of any current or ongoing name, image, and likeness compensation that exceeds $600, including multiple payments, remunerations, or benefits from the same person that exceeds a total of $600 over a 12-month period.

Small deals need no report

Deals that pay less than $600 over 12 months from one source do not have to be reported. The $600 amount goes up with inflation, which is the rise in prices. The bill uses the rise in the Consumer Price Index over the latest 12 months. That index is a government measure of prices.

“totals less than $600 over a 12-month period”

The bill’s words, section 101(b)(3)
(3) Exception.—(A) In general.—Paragraphs (1) and (2) shall not apply to either marketing or earning compensation for the value of the name, image, and likeness rights of a student athlete or to the compensation within a name, image, and likeness agreement in which a student athlete receives less than $600, including multiple payments, remunerations, or benefits from the same person that totals less than $600 over a 12-month period.(B) Adjustment for inflation.—The amount described in subparagraph (A) shall be adjusted for inflation by the percent increase, if any, in the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics of the Department of Labor for the most recent 12-month period for which applicable data is available.

Schools must keep the reports private

A school may not release anything in these reports that would show who a player is. It needs written permission first. The permission can come from the player or the player’s agent. If the player is a minor, it can come from a parent or guardian. There are two exceptions. One is the deal database set up in section 104 (not shown here). The other is what the school must release to follow this law.

“without the express written consent”

The bill’s words, section 101(b)(4)
(4) Release of information.—Except as provided in section 104 or as necessary to comply with this Act, an institution may not release any information that would identify a student athlete or prospective student athlete in a disclosure under paragraph (1) or (2) without the express written consent of the student athlete, prospective student athlete, athlete agent of the student athlete or prospective student athlete, or, in case of a minor, the parent or legal guardian of the minor.

Reporting applies only in Division I

Only players and recruits at Division I schools must report. The Congressional Research Service says Division I is made up of the schools with the largest sports budgets.

“whose primary membership is in Division I”

The bill’s words, section 101(b)(5)
(5) Limitation.—This subsection shall apply only to student athletes and prospective student athletes of institutions whose primary membership is in Division I, as defined by bylaw 20.9 of the National Collegiate Athletic Association, or a successor bylaw.

Schools may pay players directly

Schools may pay players for the use of their name, image or likeness. So may conferences, groups like the NCAA, booster groups, others tied to a school, and outside businesses. They may also help set up such pay. The words “subject to the requirements of this title” mean this pay must still follow the rest of Title I, including the pay cap.

“may pay, provide, or facilitate compensation to a student athlete”

The bill’s words, section 101(c)
(c) Name, image, or likeness compensation by institutions.—Subject to the requirements of this title, an institution, intercollegiate athletic association, conference, collective, associated entity, or third party, may pay, provide, or facilitate compensation to a student athlete for the use of the name, image, or likeness of the student athlete.

Players may hire an agent or a lawyer

Players and recruits may hire an agent or a lawyer. Schools, conferences and groups like the NCAA may not keep them out of games or other sports events for doing so.

“having obtained an athlete agent or legal representative”

The bill’s words, section 101(d)
(d) Right to representation.—An institution, intercollegiate athletic association, or conference may not restrict the eligibility for intercollegiate athletic competition, or any other event or activity relating to intercollegiate athletic competition, of a prospective student athlete or student athlete based on the prospective student athlete or student athlete having obtained an athlete agent or legal representative.

Players get guides to these rules

Groups like the NCAA must give players and recruits, and their parents or guardians, materials that explain this part of the bill.

“educational materials relating to this title”

The bill’s words, section 101(e)
(e) Educational resources regarding this title.—An intercollegiate athletic association shall provide student athletes and prospective student athletes and the parents or guardians of student athletes or prospective student athletes with educational materials relating to this title.

Section 106. Medical coverage requirements

Who pays for players’ medical care

The bill puts this duty on each Division I school. Division II and III schools have it too, but only for their players who compete in Division I. The bill also names a conference or association made up of Division I schools as one that can carry it out. The words “provide or cause to be provided” mean they can pay the costs below themselves or have someone else pay.

“shall provide or cause to be provided”

The bill’s words, section 106(a)
(a) In general.—Each Division I institution, as defined by bylaw 20.9 of the National Collegiate Athletic Association, or a successor bylaw, each institution whose primary membership is in Division II or Division III (as so defined) only with respect to student athletes of the institution who compete in Division I, or an intercollegiate athletic association or conference comprised of Division I member institutions shall provide or cause to be provided—

Medical costs covered while playing

While a player is on a team, four things must be covered. First is all out-of-pocket costs for injuries or illness that come from playing. Those are costs the player’s health plan leaves them to pay, such as copays and deductibles. Second is the cost of a second opinion from a doctor who is not tied to the school. Third is insurance for very serious injuries or illness from playing that cost more than $90,000 to treat. Fourth is a physical exam at the end of college to record any injury or condition from playing.

“all out-of-pocket medical expenses, such as copayments or deductibles”

“exceeds $90,000 in medical costs”

The bill’s words, section 106(a)(1)
(1) during the participation of a student athlete in an intercollegiate sport—(A) all out-of-pocket medical expenses, such as copayments or deductibles, for the health care coverage of a student athlete for any injury or disease incurred through participation in an intercollegiate sport;(B) the expense for obtaining a medical second opinion independent of the institution for any injury or disease the student athlete incurred through participation in an intercollegiate sport;(C) catastrophic injury medical insurance for any injury or disease incurred through participation in an intercollegiate sport that exceeds $90,000 in medical costs; and(D) an end-of-college physical examination for a student athlete for the purpose of documenting and diagnosing any injury or condition related to the student athlete’s participation in an intercollegiate sport; and

Five years of coverage after college

Some players are covered after their last game. This is for players enrolled at the school in their last year of eligibility, meaning their last year allowed to play. Their out-of-pocket medical costs for injuries or illness from playing must be covered for 5 years. The 5 years start the day after their last college game.

“for the 5-year period beginning on the day after the last intercollegiate competition”

The bill’s words, section 106(a)(2)
(2) with respect to a student athlete enrolled at the institution during their last year of eligibility to participate in intercollegiate athletic competition, for the 5-year period beginning on the day after the last intercollegiate competition for the student athlete, the cost of all out-of-pocket medical expenses of the student athlete for health care coverage for any injury or disease incurred through participation in an intercollegiate sport.

A fund for small schools and long-term harm

College sports associations such as the NCAA must set up a fund or program. It does two things. First, it helps some Division I schools pay for the 5 years of coverage after college. These are schools that made less than $20 million from sports in the past school year. They must show they are in financial hardship, meaning short of money. Division II and III schools that choose to give that coverage can get this help too. Second, the fund pays medical costs after college for players found to have serious long-term conditions from their sport. The bill names chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE). It also names “any other cognitive impairment,” which means a problem with thinking or memory.

“less than $20,000,000 in total annual athletics revenue”

“including chronic traumatic encephalopathy and any other cognitive impairment”

The bill’s words, section 106(b)(1)
(b) Intercollegiate athletic association post-eligibility insurance and catastrophic injury fund or program.—(1) In general.—An intercollegiate athletic association comprised of member institutions that compete in Division I, Division II, or Division III, as defined by bylaw 20 of the National Collegiate Athletic Association, or a successor bylaw, on behalf of its member institutions must establish a fund or program to help cover the cost of—(A) in the case of a Division I institution that generates less than $20,000,000 in total annual athletics revenue during the preceding academic year, compliance with subsection (a)(2) (or, in the case of a Division II or Division III institution, voluntary compliance with subsection (a)(2)), in the event of demonstrated financial hardship; and(B) post-eligibility medical expenses for the student athletes of a member institution who are diagnosed with significant long-term conditions related to their participation in an intercollegiate sport, including chronic traumatic encephalopathy and any other cognitive impairment.

How much money the fund must hold

The fund must hold at least $60 million on the first day of each school year. If it runs out in a year, the next year’s amount goes up by $5 million. The amount may never go over $100 million.

“not less than $60,000,000 on the first day of each academic year”

The bill’s words, section 106(b)(2)
(2) Amount of fund.—(A) In general.—Subject to increases under subparagraph (B) and the limitation under subparagraph (C), the intercollegiate athletic association described in this subsection shall ensure that the fund or program established under this subsection is funded at an amount that totals not less than $60,000,000 on the first day of each academic year.(B) Subsequent increase.—Subject to the limitation under subparagraph (C), if the amount funded for the fund or program established under this subsection is depleted for an academic year, that amount for the next academic year shall be increased by $5,000,000 as compared to the amount for the previous academic year.(C) Limitation.—The amount funded for the fund or program established under this subsection shall not exceed $100,000,000.

TV money can pay for the fund

The association may pay for the fund with money from media rights, such as TV deals, that schools sell as a group. Section 203 (not shown here) sets up those group deals.

“may use the collective media rights revenue”

The bill’s words, section 106(b)(3)
(3) Use of collective media rights.—In ensuring that the fund or program established under paragraph (2) is adequately funded, an intercollegiate athletic association may use the collective media rights revenue from a covered entity, in accordance with section 5(d)(3) of the Sports Broadcasting Act of 1961, as added by section 203.

Schools may cover more than this

Nothing here stops schools, conferences or associations from giving players more medical coverage than this section requires. An association may also put more than $60 million in the fund in a year. The fund still may not go over $100 million.

“medical coverage in addition to the medical coverage required by subsection (a)”

The bill’s words, section 106(c)
(c) Rule of construction.—Nothing in this section may be construed to preclude—(1) an intercollegiate athletic association, conference, or institution from providing or causing to be provided to student athletes medical coverage in addition to the medical coverage required by subsection (a); or(2) an intercollegiate athletic association from exceeding $60,000,000 for the fund or program established in subsection (b) for any academic year.

Section 112. Transfer protections

Players may switch schools once and play right away

Schools, conferences and groups like the NCAA must let players transfer, or switch, between four-year colleges. A player’s first transfer has no penalty, so they can play right away. A second transfer costs the player a year. They may not play during their first school year at the new school. A player may also transfer again with no penalty when one of four things happens. Their sport is cut. Their head coach leaves. Someone tied to their school sexually assaults or harasses them. Or they go on to study for a graduate degree.

“once without losing or delaying eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports”

“a second time with a loss of eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports during the first academic year following the transfer”

The bill’s words, section 112
An institution, a conference, an intercollegiate athletic association, or any representative of such entity shall permit a student athlete to transfer from one four-year institution to another four-year institution—(1) once without losing or delaying eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports;(2) except as provided in paragraph (3), a second time with a loss of eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports during the first academic year following the transfer; and(3) additionally, without losing or delaying eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports, upon—(A) discontinuation of a sport in which the student athlete competes;(B) the departure of the head coach of the student athlete’s varsity sports team;(C) sexual assault or harassment of the student athlete by an individual associated with the student athlete’s institution; or(D) the student athlete pursuing a graduate degree.

Section 113. Eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports

Who may play Division I or II sports

A person may play on a Division I or II team if they meet four rules. They must be a “student athlete.” Section 100 defines that as a full-time student who is making satisfactory progress toward a degree. They must meet the academic standards that their association, conference or school sets for everyone. They must not be a professional athlete. And they must follow the rules that bar players for breaking state or federal law.

“meets uniform academic standards”

“is not a professional athlete”

The bill’s words, section 113(a)
(a) In general.—An individual is eligible to participate on a Division I or Division II (as defined by bylaw 20 of the National Collegiate Athletic Association, or a successor bylaw) varsity sports team or in intercollegiate athletic competition for a Division I or Division II institution (as defined by bylaw 20 of the National Collegiate Athletic Association, or a successor bylaw) if the individual—(1) is a student athlete;(2) meets uniform academic standards established by the relevant intercollegiate athletic association, conference, or institution;(3) is not a professional athlete; and(4) complies with established rules that restrict eligibility for violations of State or Federal law.

Five years to play

Players get at most 5 calendar years to compete in Division I or II. The clock starts on whichever of two days comes first. One is the day they first enroll full time at a college. The other is the first day of the school year after they turn 19. The clock does not run during time away for pregnancy, a religious mission or active military duty. Associations may add other kinds of time away, such as a serious sports injury or illness. Such a rule must apply the same way to every player.

“for a maximum of 5 calendar years”

The bill’s words, section 113(b)
(b) Years of eligibility.—(1) In general.—Subject to paragraph (2), a student athlete is eligible to compete in intercollegiate athletic competition for a Division I or Division II institution (as defined by bylaw 20 of the National Collegiate Athletic Association, or a successor bylaw) for a maximum of 5 calendar years beginning on, whichever occurs first—(A) the beginning of the academic year following the 19th birthday of the student athlete; or(B) the date the student athlete initially enrolls full time at an institution.(2) Exceptions.—Paragraph (1) does not apply during a period of absence for any of the following:(A) Reasons of pregnancy.(B) Religious mission.(C) Active-duty military service.(D) Other periods of absence, which may include serious athletic injury or medical condition, adopted by rule or bylaw by an intercollegiate athletic association that apply uniformly to all student athletes.

Drugs and betting can cost a player the right to play

Associations and conferences may limit the right to play of a player who used illegal drugs or drugs that boost performance. They may do the same to a player who bet on sports. That includes sports “event contracts,” which pay out based on how an event turns out. A school may limit the right to play of a player who broke its code of conduct for all students.

“participated in sports wagering activities or sports-related event contracts”

The bill’s words, section 113(c)
(c) Restrictions on eligibility.—(1) In general.—An intercollegiate athletic association or a conference may restrict the eligibility of a student athlete to participate in intercollegiate sports if the student athlete—(A) used an illegal or performance enhancing drug; or(B) participated in sports wagering activities or sports-related event contracts.(2) Code of conduct.—An institution may restrict the eligibility of a student athlete to participate in intercollegiate sports if the student athlete violated the code of conduct of the institution that applies to all students.

What does not make a player a pro

Two things do not make someone a professional athlete under this section. First, a basketball player is not a pro because of games they played before they turned 19 or first enrolled full time, whichever came first. Second, in sports other than football and basketball, prize money for how a player placed or performed does not by itself make them a pro. That holds before or after they enroll. But only the event’s sponsor may pay the prize. And the sponsor may not be a school, its staff or volunteers, a booster group, or anyone else tied to the school.

“shall not be considered a professional athlete based solely on the amount of prize money”

The bill’s words, section 113(d)
(d) Clarification on youth activities and prize money.—For purposes of subsection (a)(3)—(1) a student athlete who competes in basketball shall not be considered a professional athlete based on their participation in athletic competitions prior to, whichever occurs first—(A) the 19th birthday of the student athlete; or(B) the date the student athlete initially enrolls full time at an institution; and(2) a student athlete who competes in a sport other than football or basketball shall not be considered a professional athlete based solely on the amount of prize money the student athlete received based on place finish or performance in an athletic event before or after enrollment in an institution, so long as—(A) the prize money was provided only by the sponsor of the athletics event; and(B) the sponsor of the athletics event is not an institution, employee of an institution, volunteer of an institution, collective, or an associated entity.

Fair rules for players from two-year colleges

Some players move from a two-year college to a four-year school. Groups like the NCAA and conferences may not hold them to more or harder academic standards. Their standards may not be higher than those for players who move from another four-year school.

“additional or more stringent academic standards”

The bill’s words, section 113(e)
(e) Transfer from 2-year institution.—No intercollegiate athletic association or conference shall require a student athlete who transfers from a 2-year institution to a 4-year institution to meet additional or more stringent academic standards than the academic standards required for student athletes who transfer from a 4-year institution.

Schools keep their own rules

This section does not stop a school from doing three things. It may set higher academic standards for players, if all its students must meet them. It may punish a player for breaking a rule that applies to all its students. And it may punish a player for breaking a rule that applies to all its players.

“if such standards are required of all students enrolled at the institution”

The bill’s words, section 113(f)
(f) Rule of construction.—Nothing in this section shall be construed to restrict the ability of an institution to—(1) require student athletes to meet or maintain academic standards that are in addition to, or more stringent than, the standards described in subsection (a) if such standards are required of all students enrolled at the institution;(2) discipline or sanction a student athlete for violating a rule, regulation, or code of conduct that applies to all students enrolled at the institution; or(3) discipline or sanction a student athlete for violating a rule, regulation, or a code of conduct that applies to all student athletes participating in intercollegiate athletic competition at that institution.

Section 114. Prohibited compensation and agreements

No paying past the cap

This is how the cap is enforced. It covers the school, its staff and volunteers, conferences and their staff, and anyone tied to the school (section 100(2)). None of them may pay players, recruits or their families in a way that gets around the cap or puts the school over it. The retention fund and the benefits in subsection (b) are the exceptions. Their NIL deals must also pass two tests, unless the school itself makes the deal. Each deal needs a valid business purpose. And it must pay about what outside businesses pay people with similar fame who are not players at that school. Last, none of them may pay a recruit for anything before the recruit enrolls. A school may still pay the fair cost of going to a training camp that is open to people who are not recruits.

“compensation in an amount that would circumvent or result in the institution exceeding the revenue share cap”

The bill’s words, section 114(a)(1)
(a) Certain agreements and compensation prohibited.—(1) Prohibition.—An institution, an employee of an institution, a volunteer of an institution, a conference, an employee of a conference, or an associated entity shall not—(A) except as provided in paragraph (2) and subsection (b), arrange, provide, offer, or permit, directly or indirectly, compensation in an amount that would circumvent or result in the institution exceeding the revenue share cap to—(i) a student athlete, a group of student athletes, or their family members; or(ii) a prospective student athlete, a group of prospective student athletes, or their family members;(B) except for a name, image, and likeness agreement between a student athlete and an institution or between a prospective student athlete and an institution, enter into a name, image, and likeness agreement with a student athlete or prospective student athlete that is not—(i) for a valid business purpose; and(ii) commensurate with compensation paid by third parties to individuals with a similar profile, reputation, or notability who are not student athletes or prospective student athletes at the institution; or(C) provide compensation for any purpose to a prospective student athlete prior to enrollment in an institution, but nothing in this subparagraph shall prohibit an institution from providing compensation to attend a development camp or program if that camp or program is open to non-prospective student athletes and such compensation does not exceed the reasonable costs of attendance.

Extra money to keep current players

Schools get a “retention fund,” money to keep players they already have. For 9 years after the bill becomes law, a school may spend up to $22.5 million a year above the cap. It may use this only to keep players who have played at least one full season there. A school may go up to $5 million a year more over the cap. That extra amount is in proportion to what it pays players in sports that do not bring in money. The bill says these include women’s and Olympic sports. A school may still pay those players more than $5 million if it follows the cap and fund rules. A school that misses its association’s or conference’s goals for graduation rates and academic progress may not use the fund. Under section 115 (not shown here), the cap and this fund end if the House settlement ends, unless Congress passes a law to keep them.

“by not more than $22,500,000 per academic year”

“during the nine-year period beginning on the date of the enactment of this Act”

The bill’s words, section 114(a)(2)
(2) Retention fund.—(A) Retention fund exception.—Except as provided in subparagraph (B), an institution may exceed the revenue share cap by not more than $22,500,000 per academic year for the purpose of retaining a student athlete or group of student athletes who have spent at least one full competitive season at the institution.(B) Non-revenue generating intercollegiate sports programs.—(i) In general.—An institution may further exceed the revenue share cap for the purpose described in subparagraph (A) by up to an additional $5,000,000 per academic year in proportion to the amount of name, image, and likeness compensation provided by the institution through the revenue share cap described in subsection (a)(1) and the retention fund amount described in subparagraph (A) to a student athlete or group of student athletes competing in non-revenue generating intercollegiate sports programs, including women's and Olympic intercollegiate sports programs.(ii) Clarification.—Nothing in clause (i) shall be interpreted to preclude an institution from providing name, image, and likeness compensation through the revenue share cap described in subsection (a)(1) and the retention fund amount described in subparagraph (A) in excess of $5,000,000 to a student athlete or group of student athletes competing in non-revenue generating intercollegiate sports programs, including women's Benefits schools may not block

Associations, conferences and schools may not stop a player from getting certain benefits linked to school or sports. The bill ties this rule to the House settlement. The benefits can come from an association, a school, a conference or anyone tied to the school. Each must be reasonable. The first kind is travel and a short stay for family when the player is competing or has a documented physical or mental health problem. The second is meals, housing and medical costs the school does not already cover. The third is school costs such as fees and books that the school does not already pay.

“reasonable costs of transportation and temporary lodging for family members”

The bill’s words, section 114(b)
(b) Personal athletic and education benefits permitted.—An intercollegiate athletic association, a conference, an institution, or any representative thereof shall not, pursuant to the Injunctive Relief Settlement Agreement approved by the court in “In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation”, No. 20–cv–03919 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2025), restrict the ability of a student athlete enrolled at an institution to receive compensation from an intercollegiate athletic association, an institution, a conference, or an associated entity, for personal benefits related to education or intercollegiate athletics, if those benefits are—(1) reasonable costs of transportation and temporary lodging for family members of a student athlete while the student athlete is experiencing a documented physical or mental health concern or participating in an intercollegiate athletic competition;(2) reasonable costs for meals, shelter, medical coverage, and medical expenses not provided or covered by the institution; or(3) reasonable education-related financial benefits, such as institution fees, books, or other incidental educational expenses that are not otherwise provided by the institution.

Proof the money is not the school’s

Some deals involve, or are set up by, businesses that already work with the school. These are a company that holds media rights, the school’s sponsors, its clothing company, and firms that sell to its sports department. For these deals, the business must sign a statement that the money is its own and not the school’s. It must also state that the school did not give it money for the deal or let it skip money it owed the school. And it must state that the school did not set the player’s pay. The statement goes to the association or its enforcement group. If that group asks, the school must sign one too. The school’s statement says it is not using the deal to get around the cap or setting the pay.

“is not the originating source of the compensation”

“did not negotiate for and determine the amount of compensation”

The bill’s words, section 114(c)
(c) Multimedia rights holders, sponsors, apparel companies, and vendors.—In the case of a name, image, and likeness agreement involving, or brokered by, a multimedia rights holder, or a sponsor of the institution in which the student athlete is enrolled or plans to enroll that has a commercial relationship with the institution, an apparel company that has a commercial relationship with the institution, or a vendor for the athletic department or athletic facilities that has a commercial relationship with the institution—(1) the multimedia rights holder shall provide a signed certification to the intercollegiate athletic association or its designated enforcement entity affirming that the institution in which the student athlete is enrolled or plans to enroll is not the originating source of the compensation to be paid to the student athlete or prospective student athlete and that payment of the compensation is not coming directly or indirectly from the institution;(2) the third party or the sponsor, apparel company, or vendor benefitting from the name, image, and likeness of the student athlete or prospective student athlete shall provide a signed certification to the intercollegiate athletic association or its designated enforcement entity affirming that it is the originating source of the compensation to be paid to the student athlete, that it did not receive funds, directly or indirectly, from the institution in which the student athlete is enrolled or plans to enroll for the purpose of financing the agreement, that the institution did not forgo funds due from the third party or the sponsor, apparel company, or vendor for the purpose of financing the agreement, and that the institution did not negotiate for and determine the amount of compensation to be paid to the student athlete or prospective student athlete through the agreement; and(3) the institution at which the student athlete is enrolled or plans to enroll, if required by the intercollegiate athletic association or its designated enforcement entity, shall provide a signed certification to the intercollegiate athletic association or its designated enforcement entity that the institution is not circumventing the revenue share cap through the name, image, and likeness agreement, or negotiating for and determining the amount of compensation to be paid to the student athlete or prospective student athlete through the agreement.

Outside deals need a real business reason

Every NIL deal must have a valid business purpose, except a deal with the school itself. Section 100(22) defines that term. It means the deal truly promotes goods or services sold to the public for profit.

“shall be for a valid business purpose”

The bill’s words, section 114(d)
(d) Valid business purpose.—Except for a name, image, and likeness agreement with an institution, a name, image, and likeness agreement with a student athlete shall be for a valid business purpose.

A limit on where big coach pay comes from

This rule is for schools that made more than $80 million from sports in the past school year. Such a school may not pay a coach or team manager more than $500,000 out of other money. It may use only two sources for that pay. One is money from college sports. The other is gifts to the sports department. The rule does not cap coach pay that comes from those two sources.

“any source other than college sports revenue or a donation or contribution to the athletic department”

The bill’s words, section 114(e)
(e) Payments to coaches and managers.—(1) In general.—An institution described in paragraph (2) may not compensate or otherwise provide or promise any type of payment or benefit of more than $500,000 to any coach, assistant coach, general manager, or other person who coaches or manages a varsity sports team by using, diverting, budgeting, or otherwise obtaining funds from any source other than college sports revenue or a donation or contribution to the athletic department of the institution.(2) Institution described.—An institution described in this paragraph is an institution with more than $80,000,000 in total annual athletics revenue during the preceding academic year.

Which schools these rules cover

In this section, “institution” means a school that meets two tests. It is a college under the Higher Education Act. And it is part of the House settlement, through its conference or in some other way, or it has opted into the settlement. So the cap, the retention fund, the deal rules and the coach pay limit in this section reach only schools in the settlement.

“has opted in to the Injunctive Relief Settlement in that case”

The bill’s words, section 114(f)
(f) Institution defined.—In this section, the term “institution” means—(1) an institution of higher education, as that term is defined in section 101 of the Higher Education Act of 1965 (20 U.S.C. 1001); and(2) an institution that is a party to, through membership in a conference or otherwise, “In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation”, No. 20–cv–03919 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2025), or has opted in to the Injunctive Relief Settlement in that case.

Section 118. Limitation on liability

A shield from antitrust lawsuits

Antitrust law, such as the Sherman Act, generally bans deals that unfairly limit trade. Players have used it to sue the NCAA over limits on their pay. This part says the NCAA, conferences and schools do not break antitrust law when they enforce or follow certain rules in the bill. These are the rules on the pay cap and retention fund (sections 114 and 115), who may play (113), transfers (112), recruiting (117), agents (103) and coaches who switch schools mid-season (110). Some NIL rules in section 101 are covered too. So are some rules on which schools are picked for championships and tournaments.

“It shall not be unlawful under the antitrust laws”

The bill’s words, section 118(a)
(a) In general.—It shall not be unlawful under the antitrust laws for an intercollegiate athletic association, a conference, or an institution to enforce or comply with, including through rules or bylaws—(1) section 114;(2) section 115;(3) section 113;(4) sections 101(a)(3) and 101(b)(1) and subparagraphs (A) and (B) of section 101(b)(2);(5) section 117;(6) section 112;(7) rules, bylaws, or requirements of an intercollegiate athletic association that determine whether a specific institution is selected to participate in a championship or tournament if the process for selecting participants is not entitled to antitrust exemption under this subsection;(8) section 103; and(9) section 110.

Penalties are shielded too

Associations and conferences also do not break antitrust law when they punish rule breakers. They may fine schools and their staff and volunteers. They may also fine conferences, their staff, and groups tied to a school. They may bar schools, school staff and volunteers, conferences and conference staff from games, including championships. These penalties are for breaking the rules on transfers, who may play, the cap, recruiting, or coaches who switch schools mid-season. Players may be barred if they cannot play under section 113, transferred against the rules, or were paid against the cap rules. And an agent who breaks the agent rules may be decertified, which means the agent loses their approval.

“impose a fine against an institution”

“decertify an athlete agent”

The bill’s words, section 118(b)
(b) Sanctions by an intercollegiate athletic association.—It shall not be unlawful under the antitrust laws for an intercollegiate athletic association or conference to, including through rules or bylaws—(1) impose a fine against an institution, an employee, or volunteer of an institution, a conference, an employee of a conference, or an associated entity for a violation of sections 110, 112, 113, 114, 115, or 117;(2) restrict an institution, employee or volunteer of an institution, a conference, or an employee of a conference, from participation in intercollegiate athletic competition, including championships or tournaments, for a violation of sections 110, 112, 113, 114, 115, or 117;(3) restrict the eligibility of a student athlete who—(A) is not eligible to participate in intercollegiate athletic competition or participate on a varsity sports team under section 113;(B) has transferred to an institution in a manner that does not comply with section 112; or(C) has received compensation in contravention of section 114 or section 115; or(4) decertify an athlete agent for violations of section 103 or the amendments made by section 102.

The shield needs written rules first

An association gets this shield only if it has written rules that put all of these provisions in place. That means every item listed in subsections (a) and (b).

“shall not be entitled to the antitrust exemptions”

The bill’s words, section 118(c)
(c) Requirements for an intercollegiate athletic association.—An intercollegiate athletic association shall not be entitled to the antitrust exemptions set forth in subsections (a) and (b) unless the intercollegiate athletic association has established rules, bylaws, or other regulations implementing paragraphs (1) through (9) of subsection (a) and paragraphs (1) through (4) of subsection (b).

An enforcement group gets the shield

An association or conference may name a group it controls under the House settlement to enforce section 114. That group then has the same shield. On the Senate floor, Sen. Cruz said this group is the College Sports Commission. Before the group puts out a new rule or guidance about the settlement, it must give fair notice to the parties sued in the House case. It must also let them comment. Those parties are the NCAA and the conferences that were sued.

“reasonable prior notice and an opportunity to comment”

The bill’s words, section 118(d)
(d) Designation of entity.—(1) In general.—An intercollegiate athletic association or conference may designate an entity under its control pursuant to the Injunctive Relief Settlement Agreement approved by the court in “In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation”, No. 20–cv–03919 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2025) for which it shall not be unlawful to enforce or comply with section 114.(2) Notice and comment requirement.—If an entity designated under paragraph (1) proposes to issue a measure, rule, guidance, or policy interpreting or implementing the Injunctive Relief Settlement Agreement approved by the court in “In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation”, No. 20–cv–03919 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2025), the designated entity shall give reasonable prior notice and an opportunity to comment to the defendant parties in “In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation”.

Section 121. Relationship to existing law

Federal rules win over state rules

When a federal law overrides state laws, it is called preemption. Here, states and local governments may not keep or pass laws that clash with Title I in a way that blocks following it. They also may not have laws that control or overturn NCAA, conference or school rules on three things. Those are NIL pay, transfers between schools, and the rules in section 113 on who may play and for how long. The Congressional Research Service says more than half the states have acted on NIL pay since 2019.

“No State or political subdivision of a State may adopt, maintain, enforce, or continue in effect any law”

The bill’s words, section 121(a)
(a) In general.—No State or political subdivision of a State may adopt, maintain, enforce, or continue in effect any law, regulation, rule, requirement, or standard that—(1) conflicts with any provision of this title that would prevent compliance with this title; or(2) governs, regulates, or invalidates policies or rules of an institution, a conference, or an intercollegiate athletic association that regulates—(A) the compensation to a student athlete or prospective student athlete for the use of their name, image, or likeness, except as necessary to comply with this title;(B) transfers of student athletes between institutions; or(C) any provision described in subsection (a)(1)-(3) or (b) of section 113 relating to the eligibility of a student athlete to participate in intercollegiate athletics.

State laws that still apply

Some kinds of state law stay in force, unless they clash with subsection (a). They are laws on sports agents, civil rights, crime, privacy and data breaches, contracts, trademarks, copyright, and consumer protection. Tort law stays too. Tort law lets people sue over harm done to them.

“Except to the extent that such law, rule, regulation, requirement, or common law right or remedy conflicts with subsection (a)”

The bill’s words, section 121(b)(1)
(b) Preservation of state laws.—The following State laws, rules, regulations, or requirements, or common law rights or remedies shall not be preempted, displaced, or supplanted:(1) Except to the extent that such law, rule, regulation, requirement, or common law right or remedy conflicts with subsection (a), the following:(A) Uniform Athlete Agent Acts.(B) Civil rights laws.(C) Tort law, unless otherwise specified in paragraph (2).(D) Criminal law, unless otherwise specified in paragraph (2).(E) Laws that relate to privacy or data breach.(F) Contract law.(G) Trademark law.(H) Copyright law.(I) Consumer protection law, unless otherwise specified in paragraph (2).

The right to sue stays

State laws that let people sue also stay, unless they clash with subsection (a)(2). They cover suits over personal injury, including harm to the mind, and wrongful death. They also cover property damage, sexual assault, harassment, fraud, abuse or hazing of a player, and poor campus safety. One more is a failure to protect players better than the health and safety rules in section 107 (not shown here) require. Claims that grow out of any of these stay too.

“Personal injury, including psychological injury.”

The bill’s words, section 121(b)(2)
(2) Except to the extent that such law, rule, regulation, or requirement, or common law right or remedy conflicts with subsection (a)(2), any law, rule, regulation, or requirement, or common law right or remedy, whether statutory or common law, that gives rise to a cause of action for the following:(A) Personal injury, including psychological injury.(B) Wrongful death.(C) Property damage.(D) Sexual assault.(E) Harassment.(F) Fraud.(G) Injury resulting from abuse or hazing of a student athlete, including physical or sexual abuse.(H) Injury resulting from deficient student or campus safety measures.(I) Injury resulting from a failure to provide greater protections to student athletes than those provided by the health, wellness, and safety standards under subsections (a)(1)-(4) or (b)(1)-(2) of section 107.(J) Any related cause of action that is derivative of any of subparagraphs (A) through (I).

Antitrust law still applies elsewhere

Some things are not shielded from antitrust law by this bill. For those, subsection (a) does not wipe out antitrust law.

“shall not be construed to preempt, displace, or supplant the antitrust laws”

The bill’s words, section 121(c)
(c) Rule of construction.—To the extent liability for violations of the antitrust laws is not limited by this title, subsection (a) shall not be construed to preempt, displace, or supplant the antitrust laws.

Section 122. Neutrality on employee or non-employee status

The bill does not decide if players are employees

The bill takes no side on whether college players are employees of their schools. It changes nothing about that question.

“This title is neutral on, and does nothing to alter, employee or non-employee status for student athletes.”

The bill’s words, section 122
This title is neutral on, and does nothing to alter, employee or non-employee status for student athletes.

Section 202. Limitation on liability for transmission of collegiate sports competitions

Schools may sell TV rights together

A 1961 law, the Sports Broadcasting Act, lets pro football, baseball, basketball and hockey leagues sell their teams’ TV rights together. They can do so without breaking antitrust law. This adds the same kind of rule for college sports. Schools and conferences may join to form a “covered entity” that sells the TV rights to their games. It must follow the rules of new sections 5 and 6 of that law. Sections 203 and 204 of this bill add those sections (not shown here).

“The antitrust laws, as defined in subsection (a), shall not apply to any joint agreement”

The bill’s words, section 202(a)
(a) In general.—Section 1 of the Sports Broadcasting Act of 1961 (15 U.S.C. 1291) is amended—(1) by striking “That the” and inserting the following:“1. Exemption of certain agreements from antitrust laws.—“(a) Professional sports.—The”; and(2) by adding at the end the following:“(b) College sports.—The antitrust laws, as defined in subsection (a), shall not apply to any joint agreement, by or among institutions engaging in or conducting organized intercollegiate sports, or conferences that have such institutions as members, to form and operate a covered entity that complies with and enforces the requirements of section 5 and sells or otherwise transfers to a third party all or any part of the rights of the institutions or conferences in the sponsored telecasting of the intercollegiate athletic competitions engaged in or conducted by the institutions or conferences.”.
